Before you start

✈️Why aircraft need fuel systems

An aircraft cannot fly without fuel. But fuel does not just sit in a tank. It must be stored safely, pushed to the engine at the right pressure, and kept clean. The fuel system does all of this, in every kind of flight, even when the aircraft turns or climbs.

🔧Why maintenance people must understand it

Technicians check, repair and test the fuel system. If you know how fuel normally flows, you can spot what is wrong: a leak, a blocked filter or a wrong gauge reading. A small fault left alone can stop an engine.

⚠️Why fuel safety is so important

Fuel vapour burns and explodes with a tiny spark. Fuel also harms skin and lungs. Safe habits protect you, your team and the aircraft: no ignition sources, bonding before refuelling, and clean-up of every spill.

    Aircraft Fuel System Overview

    The fuel system is the part of the aircraft that holds fuel and delivers it to the engine, safely, in every flight condition.

    After this page you can name the five jobs of a fuel system and explain why maintenance matters.

    The five jobs of a fuel system

    StoreTanks hold the fuel, in the wings and body.
    SupplyPumps and pipes push fuel to the engine.
    TransferFuel moves between tanks to keep the aircraft balanced.
    VentAir flows in as fuel leaves, so the tank is not crushed.
    MeasureProbes and gauges tell the crew how much is left.

    🔥Why fuel matters

    No fuel at the engine means no thrust. Even a short break in flow can stop the engine.

    ⚖️Why balance matters

    Fuel is heavy. If one wing carries much more than the other, the aircraft is harder to control.

    🔧Why maintenance matters

    A small leak, a blocked filter or a wrong gauge reading can turn into a fire risk or an engine stop.

    Simple words to know

    Tank
    A container (often part of the wing structure) that holds fuel.
    Boost pump
    A pump inside a tank that pushes fuel toward the engine.
    Feed tank
    A small tank that supplies the engine directly.
    Vent
    A small opening that lets air in and out of a tank.
    Valve
    A part that opens or closes to allow or stop flow.
    Contamination
    Anything that should not be in fuel, such as water or dirt.

    Fuel Quantity Indication

    The crew cannot look inside a fuel tank in flight. Instead, the aircraft measures the fuel and shows the answer in the cockpit. This is the fuel quantity indication system.

    After this module you can explain how fuel is measured, what the cockpit shows, and when the low fuel warning comes on.

    📏Fuel quantity sensors

    Long thin probes stand inside the tank. Fuel and air affect a tiny electric signal in different ways. The more of the probe that is covered by fuel, the bigger the signal.

    🧮Fuel gauges

    A small computer reads the signals and turns them into a number, such as kilograms or per cent. The gauge is the part that shows that number.

    🧑‍✈️Cockpit indications

    The pilot sees a needle or digital number for the fuel, often for each tank and a total. Some aircraft show bars, and some show it on a screen.

    🚨Low fuel warnings

    When fuel falls below a set level, a caution light comes on. At a lower level a stronger warning flashes. The crew must then plan to land.

    Why it matters for maintenance

    • Water, dirt or corrosion on a probe gives a wrong reading.
    • A reading that is too high can hide a fuel shortage. A reading that is too low wastes fuel and flight time.
    • Technicians test the warning lights and compare the gauge with a known amount of fuel.

    Why it matters

    ⚖️Why balancing is important

    Fuel is heavy. An aircraft flies best when its weight is spread evenly. Balanced fuel keeps the aircraft level and keeps its balance point (centre of gravity) in a safe place.

    📐Effect of imbalance

    The heavier wing drops, so the aircraft wants to roll that way. The pilot must keep correcting, which adds drag and uses more fuel. A big imbalance can make the aircraft hard to control, especially at low speed when landing.

    🧭Operational considerations

    Crews use fuel in a set order and watch the quantities. They transfer fuel only as the procedure allows. Maintenance checks that pumps and valves work, that quantity readings are right on both sides, and that transfers stop before a tank overflows through its vent.

    Refuelling and Defuelling Process

    Safety first. This page shows the general idea only. In real work always follow the approved procedure for your aircraft and base.

    Activity: tap the steps in the correct order. The tank fills as you get them right.

      0%
      Tank level

      Defuelling means taking fuel out of the aircraft, for example before tank repair or when the aircraft is too heavy.

      Before

      1. Same safety checks as refuelling: area, extinguisher, no ignition sources.
      2. Bond (earth) the aircraft and the bowser or tank.
      3. Confirm where the fuel will go and that there is room.

      During

      1. Connect the hose and open the correct valves.
      2. Start the flow slowly and watch for leaks.
      3. Watch the level so nothing overflows.

      After

      1. Close valves, disconnect, cap the coupling.
      2. Keep the fuel clean and labelled for re-use or disposal.
      3. Remove the bonding wire last. Record the amount.
      Remember: fuel fumes are the danger, not just liquid. Spilled fuel is cleaned up at once.
