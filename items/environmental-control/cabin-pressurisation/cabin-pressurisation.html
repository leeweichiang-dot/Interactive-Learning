Overview

Why do we pressurise the cabin?

Aircraft fly very high, often at 35,000 feet. At that height the air is too thin to breathe. A person who breathes it would soon feel dizzy and then pass out.

To keep people safe, the aircraft pumps air into the cabin and keeps it under pressure. This is called pressurisation.

How much pressure is enough?

The cabin is not kept at ground-level pressure. That would put too much strain on the aircraft body. Instead, the cabin is kept at a safe pressure equal to the air at 6,000 to 8,000 feet. This holds even when the aircraft flies at 35,000 feet.

We call this the cabin altitude. It is the height the cabin “feels like” to the people inside.

Two terms to remember

  • Cabin altitude – the height the air pressure inside the cabin matches.
  • Differential pressure – how much higher the pressure is inside the cabin than outside. The aircraft body is built to take a set maximum.

Key components at a glance

  1. Bleed air from the engines – hot, high-pressure air taken from the engines to supply the system.
  2. Air conditioning packs – machines that cool and clean the hot bleed air so it is safe to breathe.
  3. Outflow valve – a door that lets air out of the cabin to hold the right pressure.
  4. Safety valve – a backup valve that opens if the pressure gets too high or too low.
  5. Differential pressure controller – the “brain” that tells the outflow valve how far to open.
